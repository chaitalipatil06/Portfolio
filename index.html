my house recipe

How I brew a good product

  1. 1

    Find the real need, then cut to it

    Requests usually arrive as a symptom or a long wishlist. I talk to the person, have them recreate the problem, and separate what’s essential from what’s nice to have. Then I ship the essential part fast.

    In practice: An SSN field had to behave exactly like the legacy app: focus on open, numbers only. The vendor’s component could do one or the other but not both, and their support never replied. I listed the requirements with the team, ranked them, and rebuilt the field in plain HTML and CSS around the must-haves. Editing digits mid-number was set aside as non-essential so the fix could ship.

  2. 2

    Data first, then the picture

    I don’t sketch until I know what the data can support. Then I write a short flow covering the problem, what users need, and what it takes to build. I pair it with a quick before-and-after mockup so everyone reacts to the same thing.

    In practice: For a new equipment health feature, I spent days in spreadsheets working out which readings and formulas could produce a meaningful score, before drawing a single screen. That groundwork is what made the mockup solid enough to show a key customer.

  3. 3

    Design for both sides of the screen

    Many features serve two groups: one person who watches the numbers and another who lives with them. I design so one doesn’t pay for the other, and I plan for people who see color differently from day one.

    In practice: Support managers wanted average ticket handling times, but a visible timer would push agents to rush troubleshooting. I proposed tracking that starts quietly when a ticket opens, so managers get their data and agents stay focused on quality. In the charts I’m specifying now, lines differ by pattern, not just color, and I avoid hard-to-separate pairs like purple and dark blue.

  4. 4

    Own it past the handoff

    On a small team, waiting is the most expensive step. I learn the tool, take the task, test it myself, and keep watching after release.

    In practice: I’m learning Ignition so I can take basic development and maintenance tasks off a one-developer team. I join device bench tests so I can write test steps from what I see firsthand. After release, I retest and watch incoming support tickets for patterns, which tells me when an implementation needs another look.

Product design · Installable web app · Built with Claude

SavePay Tracker

A budgeting app that tells you what you can still spend before payday, without ever asking for a bank login.

Read the story
The problem
Budgeting apps want bank access before they tell you anything useful. I just wanted to know how much I could spend on fun and still hit my savings goals.
What I did
Wrote the full product spec and built the app with Claude: the first-run setup, spending bars that turn orange at 50% and red at 85%, per-paycheck savings math and reminders. I made the trade-off calls: an installable web app instead of App Store fees, receipt reading on the phone instead of a paid cloud AI that would send receipts off the device, and no accounts, so data never leaves the phone. Then I tested it on my own phone and worked through install and camera issues as they came up.
What it shows
I can take a problem all the way to a live product and use AI to move fast while making the product decisions myself. Accessibility was built in from day one: a low-vision-friendly font, and color is never the only signal. Using it daily changed my own spending habits.

Virtual Paint Board

Draw in mid-air with hand gestures and a webcam. No mouse, no stylus, no touchscreen.

Read the story
The problem
Drawing on a screen usually needs a mouse, stylus or touchscreen. We wanted to see whether a plain webcam and your hand could be enough.
What we built
A gesture-controlled canvas that tracks 21 points on the hand. One finger up draws, two fingers erase, and an open hand picks a shape, so switching tools never needs a menu.
What it shows
I enjoy turning a messy, real-time input like a moving hand into an interaction that feels simple to use.

Back of House

An in-house app for the coffee shop I plan to open: staff check-in, stock, reordering, a weather-aware daily menu and recipes.

Read the story
The problem
A small café juggles staff, stock, orders and a menu that should change with the weather and what's on the shelf. New baristas also need to learn fast.
What I did
Defined it as one connected system instead of separate tools. Stock builds the reorder list, stock plus weather suggests the day's menu, and every drink links to step-by-step recipes with a practice mode for new staff. I set the design rules: soft pastels with one color per section, a highly readable font, WCAG-checked contrast, and one line of plain help on every screen. The menu always keeps a sugar-free drink and an energy drink when stock allows.
What it shows
I design for everyone who touches a tool, including the newest person on shift, and I think in systems, not single screens.
Where it's at
The core app is built and passes its tests and accessibility scan. A live demo is next.
